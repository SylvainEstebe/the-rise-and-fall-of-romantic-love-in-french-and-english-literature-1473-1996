The Viceroy, who was already accustomed to receiving a visit from her dear Galand every morning, awaited him that day with more impatience than ever, and was almost irritated with him for his delay, to the point of becoming angry, as if he could have guessed the favorable disposition her heart held for him that day. She could not wait any longer, and as soon as she had finished dinner, she had to go to Donna Angelica's to learn the news. She was told that he had spent part of the morning there, which caused her a cruel resentment, adding to all her other sorrows...put them in a very bad mood for the rest of the day. The Count only arrived at her house in the evening, and found her in her room very sad and very dreamy. She was doubtless thinking of him. She received him with a coldness that made him realize she had some complaint to make, and it wasn't long before he knew what it was, for after pressing him a little, she made a thousand reproaches about several things for which he was not guilty, and which finally led to the visit he had paid that day to Donna Angelica, about which she made some mocking remarks. The Count replied that even if he loved this Lady, he would not make her obey him. "You are a traitor," she replied. "You obeyed me without hesitation. It is all too clear that I advised you to do nothing that did not align with your own inclinations, and that by refusing your heart, I prevented you from betraying me. You have...""So much power still, Madam," replied the Count, "on this subject, that if you would only repent of the gift you have made of it, it would effortlessly return entirely to you." It was indeed well-deserved to be called a traitor to speak to her in that manner; but he had reasons for it, and he feared the Viceroy's mind as much as the Viceroy's. He had clearly seen that she had only persuaded him to love Donna Angelica, whom she mortally hated, in order to ruin her. Moreover, as he did not know what was going on in the soul of this beauty, although he had recently discovered in her a little more inclination towards him, he did not believe that what he was saying would have such great consequences; but the Viceroy, who truly imagined that the Count had more affection for her than for his rival, had little difficulty in being persuaded of this, andShe even encouraged him to speak sweetly to her. This Cavalier, who was in a gallant mood that day after the happy morning he had spent, added perfidy upon perfidy, and said to her all that he could have said to Donna Angelica; so that the deluded Viceroy Donna fell for it, and was completely charmed by it. These kinds of betrayals are very common today among both men and women. That is why people are more vigilant and no longer trust words any more than they trust eyes. Everything lies, and even the heart is often a great liar; what it wants today, it no longer wants tomorrow. The young Count felt a little of the corruption of the age: and the Viceroy, although otherwise a very clever woman, let herself be deceived because she extremely wished that things were as this young dandy told her. He saw Donna Angelica, but only in private, and upon leaving her, he would rush, filled with love, to pay court to the Viceroy. The same was true of the Viceroy, whom Donna Angelica treated somewhat more favorably to amuse him and not arouse his suspicion that she was having a secret affair with Count Henry. But a jealous man or woman cannot be deceived for long by those whom love sometimes takes pleasure in blinding. It is difficult, when one has a great passion, to always be on the defensive against another passion; and if one forgets oneself in the presence of those who are watching over us, one is quickly brought back to reality. The Viceroy and Donna Angelica hardly saw each other anymore. The jealousy that had already strained their relationship over the husband had further divided them over the Galand. As for the Viceroy, he was pleased with theThe Count, on matters concerning his wife, but not on the side of his mistress, and while he was at rest from the importunity of the first, he found himself in anxiety for the love of the other. This supplicating action of the Neapolitan Count, or he had seen it in the garden, came back to him incessantly, and he had noticed since that time such a great change in the way of life of Donna Angelica, who sometimes slipped away from home for half a day, without anyone knowing where she went, that it gave him much to think about. The Viceroy was much happier than he was. She believed she was loved by the Count as much as she loved him. She saw him every day and enjoyed with him all the freedom that women who don't care about being known to have a lover are wont to take. She was within her rights to do so, and even to do worse.without anyone being able to find fault with it. Her husband wanted it: it was a Galand she had received from his own hand, and whom he had taken the trouble to train himself to please her. It is true that it went to certain limits, but that was the joy of the Viceroy because it still left her the means to take revenge on him, which was to not give in to his desires herself. When inclination is combined with vengeance, one rarely misses one's mark; and women especially, who always have revenge ready. The Viceroy found herself one morning in a very foul mood, unwilling to forgive the Viceroy. She had had a thousand pleasant dreams about the Count during the night, and she was perhaps only waiting for the moment to see him to turn them into reality. "There," she said, "is my treacherous husband gone; I am alone, and if the Count were to come now, I don't know what..."which I would not do; but I know well that there are few women in the world who have as much patience as I. What, mistreated on all sides by a man who should adore me, and who maintains a flirtatious relationship before my very eyes? Who does even worse, good heavens! Who seeks me a gallant to console me for his perfidy, or rather to amuse me, who teaches him how to please me, and who complains that he does not please me enough. Ah! If only the most honest women had husbands like mine! We would see if their honor could withstand such cruel insults. From these thoughts she passed to more pleasant ones. She pictured the Count in a hundred charming ways, which filled her with amorous impatience to see him arrive. But all her desires and all her languorous movements did not bring her there: midday was already passing and she was no longer preparing herself.than to make a thousand reproaches to him, being almost as angry with him as with the Viceroy, although very differently. The unfortunate Count was then quite distressed and hardly gave her a thought. She sent him to look for her twenty times at his house and at Donna Angelica's, without being able to get any news.
