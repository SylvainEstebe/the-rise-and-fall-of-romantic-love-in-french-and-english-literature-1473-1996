The rise and fall of romantic love in French and English literature, 1473–1996
Sylvain Estebe¹, Edgar Dubourg²˒³, Ze Fu², Nicolas Baumard²
¹ Center for Humanities Computing, School of Culture and Society, Aarhus University, 8000 Aarhus C, Denmark.
² Institut Jean Nicod, Département d’études cognitives, École normale supérieure, Université PSL, EHESS, CNRS, 75005 Paris, France.

Abstract
Romantic love is found in nearly all human societies, yet the importance people give it varies widely across cultures and periods. Historians and anthropologists have argued that love became more important as societies modernized, and cross-national surveys show that it matters more today in more developed countries. Recent studies, however, suggest that love, or some of its components, loses importance in the most developed societies. How can these two observations be reconciled? Here we examine the period that connects them. Using a large language model, we measured the narrative importance of romantic courtship in 5,758 French and English works of fiction published between 1473 and 1996, annotating the texts themselves rather than plot summaries. Courtship gained importance until the second half of the seventeenth century, then lost it, and the decline was steepest during the nineteenth century. This decline came mainly from love stories giving courtship less room, not from fewer works telling love stories. These results show that the decline of courtship in fiction began long before the contemporary period, and are consistent with the possibility that it accompanied the onset of sustained economic growth.
1. Introduction
Romantic love appears in nearly all human societies. Ethnographers have found evidence of it in 147 of 166 cultures (Jankowiak and Fischer 1992), and in a survey across 90 countries, most respondents said they would be reluctant to commit to a long-term relationship without love (Kowal et al. 2024). Yet the importance people give to love varies widely. It differs across individuals (Bode and Kavanagh 2025) and across cultures (Sorokowski et al., 2023), and societies disagree about whether love should be the basis of marriage (Levine et al., 1995). What explains this variation? One answer is that the importance of love depends on the social and economic conditions in which people live (Baumard et al., 2022; Jackson & Atari, 2025).
Much evidence supports this answer. Historians have described how love gained importance as European societies changed. Courtly literature celebrated it in the Middle Ages (Lewis, 1936), affectionate marriage spread in early modern England (Stone, 1977), and love gradually became the legitimate basis for choosing a spouse (Coontz, 2006). This trend is not specific to Europe. Historians of literature have described a similar rise of love in the Arab world under the Abbasids (Vadet, 1968), in medieval Persia (Meisami, 2014), in Islamic India (Behl, 2016), in Heian Japan (Walter, 1994), and in Chinese fiction from the Tang dynasty onward (Hanan, 2017; Hsieh, 2008). Quantitative studies confirm this trend and link it to economic development. Across 19 regions and nearly four millennia, romantic content in literature increased during periods of economic growth: Russian literature, for instance, became markedly more romantic during the country’s economic take-off in the eighteenth century (Baumard et al., 2022). Later studies have documented similar trajectories in Chinese (Zhong et al., 2023) and Persian literature (Samani et al., 2025). The same association holds across countries today: people in more modernized countries report stronger experiences of love (Sorokowski et al., 2023).
Other findings, however, point in the opposite direction. Love themes have declined in popular song lyrics over recent decades (Madanikia & Bartholomew, 2014), and in the same cross-national survey, passion and commitment decrease beyond a high level of human development (Sorokowski et al., 2023). Love, or at least some of its components, may therefore lose importance in the most developed societies. How can these two observations be reconciled? If love became more important as societies developed, why would it become less important once they grew affluent? One possibility is that the relationship is not monotonic: love gains importance as societies escape subsistence, and loses it beyond a certain level of prosperity. Assessing this possibility requires evidence from the period that connects the two observations, between the early modern rise described by historians and the contemporary decline captured by surveys. Yet this is precisely where the evidence stops. The largest quantitative study of love in literature ends in 1800, just as sustained economic growth began in Western Europe, and it measured love from Wikipedia plot summaries rather than from the texts themselves (Baumard et al., 2022).
Fiction offers a way to fill this gap. Surveys and experiments cannot reach people who lived centuries ago, but cultural products preserve traces of how past populations thought and felt (Baumard et al., 2024). Love is a recurrent theme of fiction (Gottschall & Nordlund, 2006), and written fiction extends much further back than recorded music or film. Two recent developments now make it possible to study fiction at scale and from the texts themselves. First, large digitized corpora now cover French and English fiction from the late fifteenth to the twentieth century (Barré 2026; Langlais et al. 2021). Second, large language models can annotate long texts directly, at a scale beyond the reach of human coders (Gilardi et al. 2023; Ziems et al. 2024), and their ratings can be validated against human judgments (Dubourg et al. 2024; Rathje et al. 2024). Here we use these tools to measure the narrative importance of one component of romantic love, which we call romantic affiliation: a character’s attraction to another person and active pursuit of a new romantic relationship through courtship or flirtation (Bode & Kowal, 2026). We annotated 5,758 French and English books and plays published between 1473 and 1996, and asked whether courtship continued to gain importance after 1800, reached a plateau, or declined.


2. Methods
2.1. Datasets
To investigate long-term changes in romantic affiliation, we analysed four literary corpora that covered French and English books and theatre. Together, they comprised 5,758 annotation records covering the period from 1473 to 1996 (Table 1). As a complementary measurement, we also used works from the Babel catalogue that were rated from bibliographic information alone (1,870 French and 826 English works; Supplementary Sections S1.1 and S5.1).
2.1.1. French corpora
Books (Roland corpus)
French books belong to the Roland corpus, comprising texts digitized by Gallica, HathiTrust, and Google Books (Camps et al., 2023; Langlais et al., 2021). The catalogue contains 25,172 book identifiers included in the corpus (Table S1). Each source text was segmented into passages of approximately 15,000 characters, and one passage was randomly selected per source. Up to 100 books were then sampled without replacement within each consecutive 20-year publication interval (1500–1519, 1520–1539, …, 1980–1999). The sample used in the analysis comprised 1,879 passages corresponding to publication dates spanning 1500–1996.
Theatre (Théâtre Classique)
The French theatre corpus comprised 1,060 plays published between 1574 and 1899 and obtained from the “Théâtre Classique” collection (https://www.theatre-classique.fr/). Because plays were generally shorter than books, the complete text of each play was used for annotation.
2.1.2. English corpora
Books (EarlyPrint–Gutenberg corpus)
The English book corpus comprised 1,887 works published between 1473 and 1949. It combined 955 texts obtained through the EarlyPrint Library (https://texts.earlyprint.org/home.html) with 932 texts obtained from Project Gutenberg (https://www.gutenberg.org/). One pre-segmented passage of at least 300 words was randomly sampled per work and truncated to its first 4,000 words when necessary (Supplementary Section S1.2).
Theatre (Martins & Baumard collection)
The English theatre corpus comprised 932 plays that came from a collection combining 324 plays from the Folger Shakespeare Library’s Early Modern English Drama collection, 38 Shakespeare plays from its Shakespeare collection, and 570 plays retrieved from Early English Books Online and Eighteenth Century Collections Online (Martins & Baumard, 2020). As in the French theatre analysis, the shorter length of the plays made it feasible to submit complete texts for direct annotation.

2.2. Operationalization and LLM annotation
Romantic love is a multifaceted psychobehavioural phenomenon (Bode & Kowal, 2026). Romantic love comprises several interrelated yet partially distinct processes (Bode & Kushnick, 2021): attraction (Fisher et al., 2006), pair-bond formation and maintenance (Fletcher et al., 2015; Sternberg, 1986), and persistent preoccupation with a beloved individual (Hatfield & Sprecher, 1986). This analysis concentrates on one of these processes: the early-stage pursuit of a partner, which we define as romantic affiliation—an individual being romantically attracted to another and actively seeking a new romantic connection through courtship or flirtation. We additionally annotated a supplementary dimension, romantic signalling, whose definition and analyses are detailed in the Supplementary Materials.
We used GPT-5.4 to evaluate the narrative importance of romantic affiliation on a seven-point ordinal scale ranging from 0 (no narrative role) to 6 (a dominant theme inseparable from the meaning of the narrative). The model was provided with the construct definition and the relevant text and instructed to assign a score according to this scale. Large language models can approximate human annotations in a range of text-classification tasks (Gilardi et al., 2023; Ziems et al., 2024), but their validity has to be established for each construct and corpus; we therefore compared model ratings with human annotations (Section 2.3). The complete scoring rubric is reported in Supplementary Table S3. The model received either the complete available text of a work or a sampled passage, depending on the corpus. Roland and EarlyPrint–Gutenberg books were scored within sampled passages, whereas French and English plays were annotated using their complete available texts. The full annotation prompts are provided in Supplementary Section S2.3. To assess whether the later decline also appeared with a measurement that did not depend on passage sampling, we obtained knowledge-based ratings of Babel works from bibliographic information alone. GPT-5.4 rated French works and Claude Opus 4.8 rated a processed subset of English works. The models were instructed to return missing scores for works they could not evaluate. These ratings describe the models’ representations of known works rather than direct annotations of complete texts (Supplementary Sections S2.4 and S5.1). 
2.3. Human validation
To assess the reliability of model-generated annotations, we used 40 French literary excerpts (80 construct-level evaluations across romantic affiliation and romantic signalling; Supplementary Section S3.1). Excerpts were accompanied by contextual information. Two human coders independently rated romantic affiliation on the same 0–6 scale, without access to the model’s ratings; romantic signalling was rated by a single coder (Supplementary Table S6). Their scores were averaged and rounded to the nearest integer for comparison with GPT-5.4. The agreement between human coders and between humans and the model was evaluated using quadratic-weighted Cohen’s kappa, which accounts for the ordinal nature of the ratings and the magnitude of disagreements. Spearman’s rank correlation was used as a complementary measure to assess whether evaluators ranked texts similarly in terms of the target construct. We calculated 95% confidence intervals for both statistics using a non-parametric percentile bootstrap with 2,000 resamples of the 40 excerpts, sampled with replacement. Paired ratings were retained together within each resampled excerpt, and confidence limits were defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distributions (random seed = 42).
2.4. Statistical analysis
Our primary question was whether romantic affiliation increased continuously over time or followed a non-monotonic trajectory, with an earlier rise followed by a later decline. This distinction raises one methodological challenge. Romantic affiliation scores represent ordered categories rather than measurements on a continuous scale. To address it, we fitted Bayesian cumulative-logit ordinal regression models separately to each corpus (Bürkner & Vuorre, 2019). These models account for the ordered structure of the 0–6 scores without assuming equal distances between categories. We included author-specific random intercepts to account for similarities among works attributed to the same author. Publication year was centred on each corpus’s mean and expressed in centuries. To investigate temporal shape, we compared a linear model with a quadratic model. Predictive performance was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation (Vehtari et al., 2017). This comparison allowed us to assess whether the additional flexibility of a quadratic model improved out-of-sample prediction. However, a negative quadratic coefficient alone does not establish an increase followed by a decrease within the observed period. We therefore complemented the model comparison with the two-lines procedure (Simonsohn, 2018), which estimates separate slopes before and after a data-driven breakpoint. Because this breakpoint is chosen to maximise statistical power, the procedure indicates whether the trend changes sign but not when it does so. To evaluate evidence of opposite trends, we examined both slope directions. Slopes were assessed using two-tailed Wald z-tests with heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors; the family-wise significance level was set at α = .05, with Holm correction (Holm, 1979) applied jointly across the eight slope tests (two per corpus). To describe the shape and timing of the trajectories, including possible plateaus, we additionally fitted cubic B-splines on publication year (3–6 degrees of freedom) within the same ordinal models. All specifications were compared with leave-one-out cross-validation, with exact refitting for observations with Pareto k > 0.7. Across the specifications within two standard errors of the best-performing model, we summarised expected-score contrasts between 1500, 1650, 1800, and 1950, the year of the maximum expected score, and the first year at which the expected score fell 0.25 points below its maximum. These timing analyses were exploratory and not preregistered. For French books, we assessed sensitivity to source-text length by adding centred log₂ total segment count to both ordinal models while retaining author-specific random intercepts. To distinguish changes in the occurrence of courtship from changes in its narrative importance when present, we decomposed mean scores into the proportion of positive scores and the mean score conditional on a positive score. We plotted both components in 20-year groups and compared century-level summaries using 3,000 bootstrap resamples of author groups. For books, these summaries refer to sampled passages; for theatre, to complete available texts; and for Babel, to knowledge-based work ratings. As a threshold sensitivity analysis, we repeated the descriptive summaries for scores of at least 3, indicating a contribution to an event or character decision. The product of the two components reconstructs the overall mean only for the positive-versus-zero decomposition. We estimated the components independently using a Bernoulli-logit model for positive scores and a cumulative-logit model for scores 1–6 among positive observations. Both stages included author-specific random intercepts and their own linear time coefficient (Supplementary Section S6.4).
2.5. Reproducibility
Primary annotations were generated using GPT-5.4. Model identifiers, annotation prompts, and output schemas are documented by corpus in the Supplementary Materials. Statistical analyses were conducted in Python using Bambi (Capretto et al., 2022), PyMC (Abril-Pla et al., 2023), and ArviZ (Kumar et al., 2019) for Bayesian ordinal models; NumPy, pandas, SciPy, and Matplotlib for data processing and visualization. The two-lines procedure was implemented in R. The availability of analysis files and annotation materials is described in the Data availability and Code availability statements.
3. Results
3.1. LLM annotations show agreement with human judgments
Before analysing historical variation, we assessed agreement between GPT-5.4 and human annotations. Quadratic-weighted Cohen’s κ was .73 (95% CI [.52, .86]) between the model and the combined human ratings, compared with .74 (95% CI [.56, .86]) between the two human coders. Spearman’s rank correlations were ρ = .74 (95% CI [.49, .88]) for the human–model comparison and ρ = .78 (95% CI [.57, .91]) for the human–human comparison (Table S6). These results support the use of model-generated annotations in the subsequent analyses.
3.2. Temporal trajectories of romantic affiliation
We first investigated whether romantic affiliation followed a consistent upward trend or whether its narrative prominence shifted over time. The clearest evidence of a non-linear trajectory emerged in French books (n = 1,879). Romantic affiliation increased during the earlier part of the observed period before declining in later centuries (Figure 1). The quadratic model provided better predictive performance than the linear alternative (ΔELPD = 14.50, SE = 5.40), with a negative quadratic coefficient (β₂ = −0.24, 94% HDI [−0.32, −0.16]).
We next examined whether a similar pattern was present in the other three corpora. English theatre (n = 932) also exhibited a concave fitted trajectory, with a negative quadratic coefficient (β₂ = −0.54, 94% HDI [−0.88, −0.18]). The quadratic model improved predictive performance over the linear alternative, although the estimated improvement was more modest than in French books (ΔELPD = 3.83, SE = 2.78). 
English books (n = 1,887) showed no robust trajectory. The quadratic fit was concave (β₂ = −0.15, 94% HDI [−0.25, −0.06]). However, the linear and quadratic models had nearly identical predictive performance (ΔELPD = 0.21, SE = 2.61), providing little basis for preferring the more complex specification. 
French theatre (n = 1,060) followed a different trajectory. Romantic affiliation declined across the observed period, with a negative linear coefficient (β₁ = −0.75, 94% HDI [−1.02, −0.46]). Here, the quadratic specification did not improve predictive performance (ΔELPD = −0.52, SE = 0.27).

3.3. Changes in trend direction and their timing
Although the quadratic models describe the overall curvature of the trajectories, they do not by themselves establish that romantic affiliation increased and subsequently declined within each observed period. To examine this possibility more directly, we applied the two-lines procedure, estimating separate temporal slopes before and after a data-driven breakpoint (Table 2). Positive earlier slopes and negative later slopes were observed in French books (+0.92 and −0.69 score points per century), English books (+0.44 and −0.18), and English theatre (+1.61 and −0.39). All six slopes remained statistically significant after the Holm correction across the eight tests (largest adjusted p = .017). These results indicate a reversal in trend direction in French books, English books, and English theatre; the breakpoints in Table 2 maximise test power and do not date the reversal, which we characterise below. For French books and English theatre, these findings were broadly consistent with the ordinal model comparisons. In English books, the two-lines procedure indicated opposite slope directions despite the near-equivalence of the linear and quadratic ordinal models, but this pattern was not robust across more flexible specifications and should be interpreted cautiously. French theatre again differed. Its earlier slope was negative (−0.93 score points per century), while the later slope was not distinguishable from zero (−0.06; Holm-adjusted p = .87), indicating a decline followed by a comparatively stable period rather than a rise and fall.
Spline models characterised the timing of these reversals (Supplementary Table S10). In French books, all curved specifications were within two standard errors of the best-performing model, and all showed a decline between 1650 and 1800 (−0.43 to −0.91 score points; all 95% intervals excluded zero), indicating a net decline across the eighteenth century rather than a plateau. Across the more flexible splines (4–6 degrees of freedom), the estimated maximum fell between 1675 and 1694, and the expected score first fell 0.25 points below its maximum between 1744 and 1762 (95% intervals spanning 1712–1784). English theatre showed the most stable timing, with a maximum between 1652 and 1664 and a decline onset between 1701 and 1718 across spline specifications. In French theatre, the decline between 1650 and 1800 was robust across specifications, whereas an earlier rise depended on the specification. English books showed no trajectory that was robust across specifications, and French Babel ratings supported a decline after 1800 without determining its earlier timing.

3.4. Sensitivity to passage sampling
The later decline is unlikely to be an artefact of passage sampling. Book scores were based on randomly sampled passages, and novels grew longer over the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, so a random passage might increasingly miss courtship scenes. Three analyses argue against this explanation. First, in French books, the decline persisted after controlling for source-text length: the quadratic model retained its advantage over the linear model (ΔELPD = 10.25, SE = 4.74), and the predicted declines from 1800 to 1900 and from 1900 to 1996 changed only from 0.94 to 0.91 and from 1.10 to 1.05 score points, respectively (adjusted 95% credible intervals for signed changes [−1.10, −0.72] and [−1.22, −0.85]). Declines also persisted among the 977 texts containing 12–32 segments (Supplementary Section S6.2). Second, English plays, which were annotated as complete texts, also peaked in the seventeenth century (maximum between 1652 and 1664 across spline specifications). Because both theatre corpora end before 1900, however, they do not speak to the later decline.
Third, we obtained whole-work ratings of Babel works from bibliographic information alone, without supplying any text (1,870 French and 826 English works). In the quadratic models, these ratings also declined from 1800 to 1900 and from 1900 to 1996 in both languages, although the English estimate for the latter interval was less precise (Supplementary Section S5.1 and Figure S3). Because these ratings do not depend on passage sampling, they indicate that the later decline is not driven by book length, although they remain subject to model-familiarity and corpus-selection biases (see Limitations).
3.5. Occurrence and conditional importance of courtship
Comparing nineteenth-century with twentieth-century French book passages, the proportion of positive scores changed from 61.6% to 59.9%, whereas the mean among positive scores declined from 3.59 to 3.17 (difference −0.42, 95% author-cluster bootstrap interval [−0.68, −0.15]). The corresponding occurrence difference was uncertain (−1.7 percentage points, 95% interval [−9.0, 5.3]). French and English Babel ratings showed a similar descriptive pattern: positive-score proportions were close across the two centuries (71.3% versus 71.8%, and 76.9% versus 77.7%, respectively), while conditional means fell from 3.37 to 2.62 and from 3.79 to 2.93. These comparisons use 1800–1899 and 1900–1996; the English passage comparison ends in 1949 (Supplementary Section S6.4, Table S9 and Figures S4a–b).
Across the eighteenth to twentieth centuries, both occurrence and conditional importance declined in French book passages. Independent models spanning 1800–1996 also identified negative temporal associations for both French components, including after length adjustment (Supplementary Figures S6a–b). The independent Babel models likewise identified declining conditional importance without clear evidence of declining occurrence. French theatre also showed declining conditional importance, whereas English book passages and English theatre did not show clear declines in either component during their respective later periods. These results support reduced narrative importance among positive observations as one contributor to the decline, while leaving a role for changes in occurrence and highlighting variation across corpora.
4. Discussion
Historians and recent quantitative studies have documented the increasing importance of romance in literature over time (Baumard et al., 2022). In this work, we asked whether this increase would persist into more recent periods and across different measures, or whether we would instead observe a decline. Romantic affiliation in French books rose until the second half of the seventeenth century and declined thereafter, with the decline becoming measurable in the eighteenth century and steepening in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. English theatre followed a similar rise and fall, whereas English books showed no robust trajectory. French theatre was mainly characterised by a decline. However, this difference may partly reflect the temporal coverage of the corpus, as the French theatre corpus begins close to the seventeenth-century peak observed in French books, and flexible models suggested, but did not establish, an earlier rise. The French theatre corpus may therefore primarily capture the declining phase of a longer historical trajectory. 
4.1. Common patterns and differences across literary traditions
One of the most interesting findings is the similarity in historical trajectories across French and English literary traditions. French books and English theatre both peaked in the second half of the seventeenth century, and their declines became measurable in the eighteenth century; in English theatre, the decline was concentrated around the turn of the eighteenth century, as the later-period decomposition found no clear change after 1700 (Supplementary Section S6.4). French theatre also declined after 1650. This convergence in shape and timing raises the possibility that changes in the narrative importance of romantic courtship were not specific to one literary tradition but may have been influenced by broader historical developments affecting both societies. A further observation, comparing books and theatre, is that romantic affiliation scores are generally higher in theatre than in books across both French and English corpora, despite some similarities in their temporal trajectories. This convergence over the full historical span does not imply a uniform decline in every later period: the supplementary decomposition (Supplementary Section S6.4) found no clear declines in either component for English book passages after 1800 or English theatre after 1700. The temporal scale and measurement source therefore remain important to the interpretation.
4.2. What could explain the rise and decline?
Different mechanisms (not exclusive) could explain this rise and fall pattern. Because the decline began in the eighteenth century and steepened in the nineteenth and twentieth, these explanations must account for a gradual onset rather than a sharp break around 1800. One explanation concerns the changing production and circulation of literature. As reading publics, publishing practices, and narrative genres developed, a corpus may have contained a changing mixture of courtship-centred stories, domestic narratives, historical fiction, and other forms. The rise of the novel and the expansion of reading markets make this possibility especially pertinent to book collections extending into the nineteenth and twentieth centuries (St Clair, 2004; Watt, 1957). A second possibility is that people’s preferences for romantic expression change with their ecological and social environment. Behavioural-ecological accounts suggest that resource availability can shape people’s preference and motivation (Boon-Falleur et al., 2025). These changes may also influence what audiences find compelling in fiction (Baumard et al., 2024). One possible mechanism concerns the signalling function of courtship. When a potential partner’s intentions are uncertain, costly gifts and displays of investment can help communicate commitment (Sozou & Seymour, 2005). The value of such signals should decline as harsh environmental conditions and uncertainty decline. Evidence suggests that interpersonal trust did increase over much of the period we examine: estimated levels of perceived trustworthiness in European portraits rose between 1500 and 2000, in association with improving living standards (Safra et al., 2020). Insofar as greater security and generalized trust reduce uncertainty about a partner’s intentions, readers may have placed less value on elaborate demonstrations of devotion while continuing to value romantic love and stable relationships. Such a change would lower the narrative prominence of courtship without implying a decline in romantic love itself. Lastly, romantic affiliation captures the formation and pursuit of a new connection; it does not directly measure enduring affection, marital attachment, commitment, or sexual desire. A particularly informative hypothesis is that established forms of companionate love became more prominent without requiring stories to devote an equally large share of their plots to courtship. Historians of marriage have described substantial changes in the social value assigned to affection and partner choice (Coontz, 2006; Stone, 1977). 
The supplementary measure of romantic signalling, which captures overt and idealizing displays of devotion, bears directly on this account (Supplementary Section S4.1 and Figure S2). In the quadratic fits, signalling in French books rose and then declined, reaching its lowest values in the twentieth century. English theatre also rose and then declined, and French theatre declined across its observed period. Knowledge-based Babel ratings of signalling also declined over the nineteenth and twentieth centuries in both languages (Supplementary Figure S3). English book passages, however, showed only a shallow concave trajectory. These signalling trajectories were not subjected to the same model comparisons and two-lines tests as romantic affiliation, and the two constructs overlap conceptually. They therefore provide descriptive support for the signalling account rather than an independent test of it.
The decomposition distinguishes two descriptive sources of change: how often courtship receives a positive score and its narrative importance among positive observations. A decline driven by conditional importance, with courtship receiving less narrative room in works that still contain it, is the pattern most directly compatible with a change in audience preferences. A decline driven by occurrence, with fewer works containing courtship at all, is compatible with at least three mechanisms: readers becoming less interested in love stories, a growing readership with more diverse tastes, and the rise of competing genres such as crime or adventure. In Babel ratings, which assess whole works, the decline was mainly one of conditional importance, and French theatre also showed declining conditional importance, whereas French book passages showed changes in both components; in book passages, zero scores may also partly reflect passage sampling. Reduced conditional importance contributed to the French decline and was also evident in the century-level Babel comparisons. However, the relative contribution of occurrence varied with the period and corpus, and a stricter threshold (Supplementary Figures S5a–b) indicated that some change involved a shift towards peripheral narrative roles rather than complete absence. These findings do not uniquely identify changing audience preferences. The set of positive works also changes over time, so shifts in genre composition, readership, publishing, or model familiarity could affect either component.
4.3. Implications for understanding cultural change
More broadly, these findings contribute to ongoing discussions in historical psychology about the direction and variability of cultural change. Recent perspectives emphasize that historical changes in psychological and cultural phenomena do not necessarily follow uniform trajectories but may depend on specific historical and institutional contexts (Jackson & Atari, 2025). Our findings also complement previous research documenting a historical increase in romantic themes in Persian literature (Samani et al., 2025), suggesting that the prominence of romantic love in cultural products may follow different trajectories across historical periods and literary traditions. Similarly, research on the historical evolution of individualism has documented differences in the timing and magnitude of cultural change across Western societies (Fan et al., 2025). Taken together, these studies highlight the importance of examining long-term cultural trajectories rather than assuming that historical changes necessarily follow a continuous or universal direction. 
4.4. Limitations and future directions
Several limitations constrain the interpretations that can be drawn from these results. Historical language change, OCR errors, and differences in the model’s familiarity with literary works could also influence the resulting scores (Chang et al., 2023; Hamilton et al., 2016; van Strien et al., 2020). The surviving and digitized literary record is also selective. Changes in which authors, genres, and works were preserved or included in the available collections may contribute to the observed trajectories (Kestemont et al., 2022; Pechenick et al., 2015). Because books were annotated from sampled passages whereas plays were annotated in full, absolute annotation scores may not be directly comparable across genres, and the higher scores observed for theatre should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Future work could extend this approach to other components of romantic love. For books, a zero score may reflect the absence of courtship from the sampled passage rather than from the complete work. The source-length controls reduce concern about this particular sampling explanation but do not establish the representativeness of a single passage.

S2.3. Text-based annotation prompts
For French books, the model received a sampled passage as textual input. The scoring rubric assessed narrative importance through references to events, character decisions, and the main plot. The prompt did not ask the model to distinguish importance within the supplied passage from importance within the complete work. These annotations should therefore be interpreted as passage-based ratings, rather than direct assessments of complete books. In the prompt below, the supplied passage is referred to as a “chapter”. 
System prompt


You are a trained annotator scoring French literary texts from the fifteenth to the twentieth century for narrative constructs. Score each construct using the 0–6 ordinal scale provided below.


0—Absent: plays no role.
1—Peripheral: has no narrative consequence; the story would remain essentially unchanged without it.
2—Background: provides context or atmosphere but does not initiate or resolve events.
3—Contributing: shapes at least one plot event or character decision.
4—Significant: drives a subplot or an important turning point.
5—Central: constitutes a primary narrative driver or major character motivation.
6—Defining: constitutes the dominant theme of the work and is inseparable from its meaning.


Base each score only on evidence present in the supplied chapter. Correct obvious OCR errors mentally when necessary, but do not infer events that are not supported by the text.


User prompt


CHAPTER:
[Full chapter text]


Score each requested construct on a scale of 0 to 6.

S2.5. Model settings and code
Text-based annotations used GPT-5.4 (gpt-5.4-2026-03-05) through the OpenAI API. Knowledge-based annotations used GPT-5.4 for the French Babel works and Claude Opus 4.8 (claude-opus-4-8) through the Anthropic API for the English Babel works. All requests used a temperature of 0. For GPT-5.4, top-p, maximum output tokens, and reasoning effort were left at their defaults, and the seed was set to 0 for the French book annotations. For Claude Opus 4.8, output was limited to 512 tokens. Text-based requests scored all constructs jointly and returned one integer score from 0 to 6 per construct; knowledge-based requests scored each construct separately and returned a score together with a familiarity indicator. Annotation scripts, prompts, output schemas, and analysis code are available at https://github.com/SylvainEstebe/the-rise-and-fall-of-romantic-love-in-french-and-english-literature-1473-1996.

S5.1. Knowledge-based annotations of Babel works

The prompt required the model to indicate whether it was familiar with a work and to return missing scores when it could not evaluate its content. S6. Statistical diagnostics and sensitivity analyses
S6.1. Primary model specification and uncertainty
The primary ordinal models use a cumulative-logit link, with an author-specific random intercept in the linear, quadratic, and spline candidates. Each corpus is fitted separately, with time centred on the mean publication year and expressed in centuries. Missing or unidentified author labels are assigned identifier-specific groups, as described in Section S6.3. Models were estimated in Bambi using four chains, each with 2,000 tuning iterations and 4,000 retained posterior draws, yielding 16,000 posterior draws per model. The random seed was set to 0.
PSIS-LOO compares predictive performance at the annotation-record level; in Table S8, ΔELPD is defined as quadratic minus linear ELPD, and spline comparisons are reported in Table S10. This is not leave-one-author-out validation. Model comparison and interpretation of temporal shape are reported separately.
Parameter summaries report posterior means and 94% highest-density intervals (the ArviZ default for parameter summaries). Expected-score bands and contrasts use 95% equal-tailed intervals, which correspond directly to posterior quantiles. Trajectory bands represent pointwise 95% equal-tailed credible intervals for the expected score. For each posterior draw, expected scores are calculated by weighting score categories by their predicted probabilities, with author-specific deviations set to zero. The plotted curve is not marginalized over the author-effect distribution. These bands are neither predictive intervals for individual annotations nor simultaneous bands for the whole trajectory.
Spline specifications used the same priors, sampling settings, author structure, and prediction rules as the primary models.
Records are grouped into non-overlapping 20-year intervals defined by 20 × floor(year/20). Intervals containing at least 10 records are displayed at their mean publication year, with error bars representing ±1.96 standard errors of the mean. Ordinal models are fitted to individual records; this display threshold does not restrict their analytical samples.
S6.2. Sensitivity to source-text length in French books
Sampling an excerpt of approximately 15,000 characters covers a smaller proportion of a longer source text. We therefore tested whether source-text length accounted for the observed French romantic affiliation trajectory. All 1,879 annotation records were linked to the original sampling metadata by passage identifier; scores and publication dates matched the primary analysis. Length was approximated by the total number of generated segments recorded for each source. This measures the available digitized text and does not establish that it corresponds to a complete novel rather than an individual volume.
We added centred log₂ total segment count to both the linear and quadratic cumulative-logit models, retaining the original author labels and author-specific random intercepts (1,264 groups). A second analysis restricted the sample to the central length interval of 12–32 segments, inclusive (977 records; 734 author groups). The bounds were determined from the length quartiles, without reference to the scores. Models retained the primary Bambi priors and sampling settings: four chains, 2,000 tuning iterations and 4,000 retained draws per chain, with seed 0. The original, length-adjusted, and restricted analyses each compared linear and quadratic time terms using record-level PSIS-LOO.
Quadratic-minus-linear ΔELPD was 14.50 (SE = 5.40) in the original analysis, 10.25 (SE = 4.74) after length adjustment, and 5.81 (SE = 3.96) in the restricted sample. The adjusted quadratic coefficient was −0.214 (94% HDI [−0.292, −0.138]); the restricted-sample coefficient was −0.238 [−0.345, −0.133]. Support for the quadratic over the linear specification was less precise in the smaller restricted sample.
Posterior expected-score contrasts were calculated from the quadratic models with author-specific deviations set to zero, matching the primary figures. Adjusted predictions held length at the sample’s geometric mean (approximately 20 segments) at every year. Between 1800 and 1900, the signed changes were −0.94 (95% credible interval [−1.14, −0.75]) in the original model, −0.91 [−1.10, −0.72] with length adjustment, and −0.84 [−1.08, −0.60] in the restricted sample. Between 1900 and 1996, the corresponding changes were −1.10 [−1.26, −0.91], −1.05 [−1.22, −0.85], and −1.07 [−1.31, −0.78]. Contrast intervals are 95% equal-tailed credible intervals conditional on the quadratic specification; parameter intervals retain the manuscript’s 94% HDI convention. Restricted-sample estimates concern a different subset of texts and are not estimates of attenuation caused by adjustment.
Across the six fits, there were no divergent transitions; maximum R-hat was 1.0024, minimum bulk effective sample size was 1,483, and minimum tail effective sample size was 2,929. No observation had Pareto k above 0.7 (maximum 0.692). These checks indicate that source-text length explains little of the estimated later decline under these models. They do not establish that a single passage captures a complete work’s thematic content, nor do they separate changes in the occurrence of positive scores from changes in their conditional importance.

S6.4. Occurrence and conditional importance of courtship
This supplementary analysis separates two descriptive contributors to mean courtship scores: how often courtship is detected (component A) and how important it is among positive observations (component B). The results support a contribution of declining conditional importance, particularly in French book passages and Babel ratings.
Definitions and data
For a courtship score Y on the 0–6 scale, let p = P(Y > 0) and m = E(Y | Y > 0). The mean score is exactly E(Y) = p × m. A change between two periods can be decomposed symmetrically as ΔE(Y) = Δp × (m₀ + m₁)/2 + Δm × (p₀ + p₁)/2. The first term is the contribution of occurrence (A); the second is the contribution of conditional importance (B). These are accounting contributions, not identified causal mechanisms.

For French passages, the occurrence difference was −1.7 percentage points (95% bootstrap interval [−9.0, 5.3]), while the conditional mean fell by 0.42 points. Occurrence-difference intervals included zero in all four comparisons in Table S9; this does not demonstrate unchanged occurrence. The symmetric point decomposition assigns −0.056 score points to occurrence and −0.253 to conditional importance for French passages, for a total change of −0.309. The corresponding contributions are +0.015 and −0.538 in French Babel, and +0.025 and −0.666 in English Babel. English passages instead show +0.144 and −0.028, for a total increase of 0.116. These values summarize the observed samples and should not be read as causal percentages.
The time scale matters. From 1700–1799 to 1900–1996, French passage occurrence fell from 74.8% to 59.9%, and the mean among positive scores fell from 3.91 to 3.17. Both components therefore contribute to the longer French decline. Comparing century-wide averages also obscures variation within each century, which motivates the separate later-period models below.
Independent later-period models
We fitted a Bernoulli-logit model for whether the score was positive and a cumulative-logit model for the positive scores, with support 1–6. Each component had its own linear time coefficient and author-specific random intercepts. The two stages were fitted independently; their author effects were not jointly modelled or correlated. Time was centred and expressed in centuries. We used Bambi’s automatically scaled default priors: centred Normal priors for slopes, Half-Normal priors for author-level standard deviations, a Normal(0, 1.5) Bernoulli intercept prior, and ordered Normal threshold priors centred at −2, −1, 0, 1, and 2 with standard deviation 1 for the ordinal stage. Model-specific prior scales are reported in the code repository.
Book analyses used 1800 through the last observed year, capped at 1996; English book passages ended in 1949. Theatre analyses used 1700–1899 for French plays and 1700–1800 for English plays. French passage models were also fitted with centred log₂ source length in both stages. Source length was measured by the number of approximately 15,000-character chunks in the available source text, a proxy rather than a verified complete-book character count.
Predictions set author deviations to zero. Length-adjusted predictions additionally set centred log length to zero. These conditional, point-in-time model contrasts differ from observed century averages and should not be expected to coincide. Figures S6a–b show posterior means and pointwise 95% equal-tailed credible intervals; the French adjusted mean is dashed. Grey points use only observations within each fitted time window.
In French passages, both components declined in the independent models. After length adjustment, the predicted 1900–1996 occurrence change was −11.2 percentage points (95% credible interval [−21.1, −2.1]), and the conditional-importance change was −0.50 score points [−0.76, −0.24]. For 1800–1900, the corresponding adjusted changes were −10.3 percentage points [−18.3, −2.1] and −0.48 points [−0.72, −0.24]. The decline in both components therefore persisted after this length control.
In Babel, the 1900–1996 conditional-importance change was −0.71 points [−1.04, −0.37] in French and −0.58 [−0.97, −0.18] in English. The occurrence changes were uncertain: +2.0 percentage points [−6.9, 10.1] and +1.8 [−6.6, 9.5], respectively. French plays also showed declining conditional importance: −0.44 points [−0.64, −0.24] from 1700 to 1800 and −0.58 [−0.91, −0.27] from 1800 to 1899.
The English exceptions are material. English passage models showed no clear decline in either component: for 1900–1949, the occurrence change was +4.8 percentage points [−1.4, 10.8] and the importance change was −0.05 points [−0.27, 0.16]. English theatre likewise showed no clear change over 1700–1800: −1.7 percentage points [−9.9, 7.7] and −0.10 score points [−0.53, 0.36]. These later-period findings do not test the same temporal pattern as the manuscript’s full-history analyses.
Fourteen models were fitted: two components for each of six corpora, plus two length-adjusted French passage components. Each retained 4,000 draws per chain across four chains. Warm-up and the target acceptance probability were increased where required. Final fits had no divergent transitions; the maximum R-hat was 1.0046, the minimum bulk effective sample size was 861, and the minimum tail effective sample size was 1,199.
This analysis is exploratory. A full-history quadratic specification was not used for the French components because it smoothed over non-monotonic recent occurrence. The later-period linear models are reported as supplements to the observed trajectories. Neither model selection nor the later time windows were preregistered; the estimates do not identify a historical turning point.
Sensitivity to the definition of presence
Figures S5a–b repeat the descriptive analysis at score ≥ 3, indicating an effect on at least one event or character decision. No additional Bayesian models were fitted at this threshold. Unlike the zero-versus-positive decomposition, P(Y ≥ 3) × E(Y | Y ≥ 3) does not reconstruct the overall mean, because scores 1 and 2 also contribute.
Between the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the proportion scoring at least 3 fell from 48.4% to 41.3% in French passages, from 43.0% to 29.2% in French Babel, and from 59.6% to 44.6% in English Babel; it rose from 38.2% to 43.0% in English passages. The respective means among scores ≥ 3 changed from 4.19 to 4.03, 4.74 to 4.56, 4.48 to 3.92, and 3.93 to 3.78. In French Babel, near-similar positive-score proportions alongside a lower proportion scoring ≥ 3 are consistent with a redistribution towards peripheral roles rather than complete disappearance.
Implications for interpretation
The findings weaken an explanation in which the mean decline is caused only by the addition of texts with no detected courtship. They support reduced narrative importance among positive observations as one contributor, with a simultaneous role for occurrence in the longer French trajectory. They do not establish that the same works changed or that readers became less interested in love: the positive subset changes between periods, and genre composition, readership, publishing supply, preservation, or model familiarity could influence either component.
